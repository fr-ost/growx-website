# Draft A: The 15-minute daily routine that grows an X (Twitter) account

**Word count:** about 970 (article only)
**Contextual link:** https://www.growxapp.org/blog/twitter-reply-strategy (in the "reply early" section)

## Title options

1. The 15-Minute Daily Routine That Grows an X (Twitter) Account
2. Grow on X in 15 Minutes a Day: A Routine You Can Actually Keep
3. No Time for Twitter? A 15-Minute Daily Routine for Steady Growth on X

## Article

Most advice about growing on X (Twitter) quietly assumes you have hours to spare. Post five times a day, reply to everyone, host Spaces,
build a newsletter on the side. If you have a job, clients or a product to build, that plan lasts about a week.

This routine is built for the opposite situation. It takes about 15 minutes a day, plus a short check once a week. It will not make you go
viral on schedule, and nothing can promise a follower count. What it does is put you in front of the right people every day, which is the
part most busy accounts skip.

### First, a one-time setup

Before the daily routine, spend half an hour making your profile worth following. Many follows start with someone reading your reply and
clicking your name, so the profile has to convince them fast.

- **A bio that says who it is for and what you post.** "I help early-stage founders with pricing. Posts on SaaS pricing and sales" does
  more than a list of job titles and emoji.
- **A pinned post that shows your best work**, such as a useful thread or a post explaining what people get by following you.
- **Two or three topics.** Not ten. A clear niche helps readers, and the recommendation feed, understand what you are about.
- **A private List of 30 to 50 accounts in your niche.** Choose people who post often and get real conversations under their posts. This
  List becomes your reply feed. Nobody is notified when you add them to a private List.

### Minutes 0 to 5: post one thing

Open your notes, pick one idea and post it. That is the whole step.

The trick is that the idea should already exist. Keep a running notes file and drop things into it during the week: a question a client
asked, a mistake you made, a tool you started using, an opinion you hold that others in your niche do not. When it is time to post, you are
choosing, not inventing.

A few formats that suit busy people:

- **A question** your audience has an opinion on.
- **A short lesson** from something that happened this week.
- **A before and after**: what you used to do, what you do now, and why you changed.
- **A resource**, with one sentence on why it is worth someone's time.

Before a busy week, write three or four posts in one sitting and schedule them. Consistency beats volume. One good post a
day is plenty, and on some days a few strong replies matter more than a post.

### Minutes 5 to 12: reply early

This is the most important part of the routine, especially for small accounts.

Open your List and look for posts from the last hour or two. Pick three to five and write replies that add something: an example from your
own work, a counterpoint, a follow-up question, a resource. Skip "Great post!" and anything you could paste under a hundred different posts.
This guide to [replying on X in a way that brings followers](https://www.growxapp.org/blog/twitter-reply-strategy) goes deeper.

Why early? A reply on a fresh post has a better chance of being read while the conversation is still active, and everyone reading that post
is already interested in your topic. A good reply is a free sample of your thinking, shown to exactly the right audience. Some readers will
click through and follow.

A day-old post with hundreds of replies above yours rarely helps. Freshness is the point.

### Minutes 12 to 15: answer your replies and follow a few people

Use the last few minutes for two quick jobs.

First, answer the replies on your own posts. A person who already replied to you is the warmest possible follower.

Second, follow a handful of relevant people: someone who left a sharp reply in a thread you read, a newer account in your niche posting good
work, someone who engaged with your post. A handful means a handful. Following hundreds of accounts in bursts and unfollowing them days
later is exactly the kind of pattern X's spam rules describe, and it can get an account limited.

### Once a week: check your numbers

Daily numbers are mostly noise. Weekly numbers start to tell you something. Pick a day, open your posts from the last seven days and note a
few things in a simple spreadsheet. It takes about 20 minutes.

1. **Engagement rate per view** for each post: likes, reposts, replies and bookmarks divided by views. It lets you compare a post seen by a
   few hundred people with one seen by thousands, fairly.
2. **Topics and formats.** Did questions beat lessons? Did images beat plain text? Look for patterns, not one outlier.
3. **Follower change.** New followers minus unfollowers for the week, compared with the week before.
4. **Which replies worked.** Did any reply earn more attention than your own posts? Whose audiences responded to you?

Then make one change for next week. Only one, so you can tell whether it helped.

### What to stop doing

Part of what makes 15 minutes enough is what you drop:

- Refreshing your analytics every hour.
- Chasing trends that have nothing to do with your niche.
- Follow trains, engagement groups and bought likes.
- Stuffing posts with hashtags.
- Using automation that ignores limits or keeps going after X shows a warning.

### What to expect

This routine compounds slowly. The first weeks often feel quiet, because what you are building is easy to miss day to day: people who
recognise your name. Give it several weeks before you judge it, and let your weekly
numbers, not your mood, decide what to change.

Fifteen minutes is not much. But fifteen focused minutes, every day, add up to more than the occasional three-hour burst most of us actually
manage.

# Draft B: What I learned building a Chrome extension for X (Twitter) growth

**Word count:** about 970 (article only)
**Contextual link:** https://www.growxapp.org/blog/twitter-follow-back-score-growx (in lesson 6)
**Note:** first person as Shahriar. Edit any line about your own thinking that does not match your experience. Do not add user counts,
revenue or results.

## Title options

1. What I Learned Building a Chrome Extension for X (Twitter) Growth
2. Building a Growth Tool for X: Lessons on Safety, Privacy and Honest Marketing
3. Slow by Design: Seven Lessons From Building a Chrome Extension for X (Twitter)

## Article

I'm Shahriar Ahmed, and I build GrowX, a Chrome extension for growing an account on X (Twitter). It schedules posts, tracks
unfollowers, shows which posts work, finds fresh posts to reply to and can follow relevant people automatically. I started it because
growing on X meant juggling an auto follow tool, a scheduler and an analytics app, and many auto follow tools moved so fast they put
accounts at risk.

Building it taught me more about restraint than features. These are the lessons I would pass on to anyone building on a platform they do
not control.

### 1. The platform's limits are the real spec

On someone else's platform, the most important requirements are not on your feature list. They are the platform's limits, many of them
unpublished and subject to change.

So the core of the product is not "follow people". It is "follow people at a pace that looks like a person, and stop the moment something
looks wrong". That means random delays, regular rests, hourly and daily caps, and a guard that stops following before you
reach X's following cap. The default pace waits 30 to 75 seconds between follows and rests every 18; the slowest, for new accounts, waits
45 to 110.

The bigger lesson was about what happens when the platform pushes back. The extension slows down after any warning from X, and it stops
completely on a sign-out, a lock, a verification challenge or repeated automation warnings. It never clicks through a challenge. I would
rather someone lose a day of growth than an account, and I say plainly that no tool can promise zero risk on X.

### 2. Verify outcomes, do not assume them

A click that appears to succeed has not necessarily worked: a platform can quietly decline to apply a follow without showing an error. A
tool that assumes success keeps going, which is exactly wrong.

So the extension checks that follows actually stuck, and treats a mismatch as a reason to slow down. The rule: build feedback loops into
anything automated. Measure the outcome, not the click.

### 3. Pacing is a product decision, not a slider

It is tempting to treat speed as a slider that users set. But speed is a trade-off between growth and risk, and most people should not have
to reason about it from scratch.

That is why the extension ships named presets instead of a raw number, with Balanced as the default. The faster presets are opt-in and
labelled for aged accounts, and the fastest is meant only for accounts with X Premium. An optional warm-up raises the daily cap
gradually, optional active hours limit when it runs, and Alt+Shift+S stops everything. Good defaults protect more people than any amount of
documentation.

### 4. Local-first is a feature people can feel

The extension works inside your own signed-in x.com session. It never asks for your X password and does not use X's OAuth. Settings,
history, posts and analytics live in the browser's own storage. No GrowX server holds anyone's X data. Optional sync goes
through Chrome's own sync storage, and backups are a file you export yourself.

This has trade-offs worth naming. Scheduled posts need Chrome and the computer to be on, because they go out through your x.com tab. A post
running more than six hours late because the laptop was closed never goes out by itself; it waits as "missed" so you decide. A server-based
scheduler avoids that, and for some people it is the better choice. Saying so builds more trust than pretending otherwise.

### 5. On-device AI is private by default, and it must fail gracefully

The AI writer uses the model built into Chrome, so it runs on the user's own computer. There is no API key to manage, no per-request bill
and no draft sent to me. That let me offer AI writing without building a data pipeline I would then have to secure.

It does need a recent desktop Chrome with enough memory and disk space, and Chrome downloads the model once. When the model is unavailable,
the buttons fall back to templates and a plain thread splitter. Degrading quietly beats showing an error.

The writer is also designed not to invent facts. When a draft needs a number or a name, it leaves a {placeholder} for the user to fill in.
Fluency is not accuracy.

### 6. Use machine learning only when it earns its place

The extension scores follow candidates from 1 to 99 with plain, explainable signals such as follow ratio, recent activity, account age and
whether there is a photo and bio. On Premium, a small model (a logistic regression) learns from the user's own follow-backs. It waits for
enough settled follows, is checked against a holdout set and is blended in only when it clearly beats chance. Otherwise the simple score
stays in charge. Here is [how the follow-back score works](https://www.growxapp.org/blog/twitter-follow-back-score-growx) in detail.

A model that is confidently wrong is worse than a heuristic that is honestly simple.

### 7. Honest marketing is slower, and worth it

Marketing has been the hardest discipline. It would be easy to write "10x your followers" or post glowing quotes from people who do not
exist. I don't. The copy has no user counts, ratings or guaranteed results, and screenshots use demo data. GrowX is independent and not affiliated with X Corp, and I say that too.

It is slower. But people who install a tool expecting the truth are the ones who stay, and they are the ones who tell you what to fix.

### What I would tell another builder

Treat the platform's limits as your spec, verify outcomes, make the safe choice the default, keep data local where you can and name the
trade-offs, let AI fail gracefully, and market only what the product actually does.

# Draft C: How to measure what works on X without paying for analytics

**Word count:** about 970 (article only)
**Contextual link:** https://www.growxapp.org/tools/twitter-engagement-rate-calculator (in the engagement rate section)

## Title options

1. How to Measure What Works on X Without Paying for Analytics
2. Free X (Twitter) Analytics: A Simple System to Learn What Your Audience Wants
3. Stop Guessing on X: Measure Your Posts With a Spreadsheet and 20 Minutes a Week

## Article

You do not need a paid dashboard to learn what works on X (Twitter). The numbers that matter most already sit under every post, and a
spreadsheet plus a weekly habit turns them into decisions. Here are four things worth measuring, how to work them out for free, and how to
avoid reading too much into noise.

### Start with what X already shows you

Every post on X shows a view count, with replies, reposts, likes and bookmarks right next to it. Depending on your account type you may also
have a fuller analytics view, but everything below works with the numbers on your own posts.

Set up a sheet with one row per post and these columns: date, time posted, topic, format, views, likes, reposts, replies and bookmarks. Keep
a second tab for your follower count, one line per week.

Record each post at a fixed age, such as two days after posting, so you compare like with like.

### 1. Engagement rate per view

Raw likes mostly measure reach. A post shown to many people collects more likes than one shown to a few, even if a smaller share of its
readers cared. Engagement rate per view removes that bias:

**Engagement rate = (likes + reposts + replies + bookmarks) / views x 100**

A made-up example: 40 engagements on 2,000 views is a 2% engagement rate. One spreadsheet formula handles it for every row, and a free
[engagement rate calculator](https://www.growxapp.org/tools/twitter-engagement-rate-calculator) is handy for a quick check on a single post.
If views are missing, dividing by your follower count works as a rough fallback, although it mixes reach and reaction.

What is a good rate? There is no universal benchmark worth trusting, because rates vary with niche, account size and type of post. The
useful comparison is with yourself. Work out your median rate over the last month and treat anything clearly above it as a post worth
learning from. Use the median rather than the average, because one viral post can drag the average up and make everything else look like a
failure.

### 2. Topics and formats

Once you have 20 or 30 posts in the sheet, tag each one with a topic (your two or three main themes) and a format: question, image, plain
text, thread, link or list. Then use a filter or a pivot table to see the median engagement rate for each tag.

Patterns worth looking for:

- A topic that keeps beating your median, which is a signal to post about it more often.
- A format that works well for one topic and badly for another.
- Posts with a link in the post compared with posts that put the link in a reply. Many creators believe links reduce reach, but there is no
  published rule, so test it on your own account.

Ignore any pattern built on two or three posts.

### 3. Your best time to post, from your own data

Generic "best time to post" charts describe other people's audiences. Yours may live in different time zones or keep different hours.

Add the weekday and hour of each post to the sheet, then group posts into time blocks (morning, midday, evening, late night) and compare the
median engagement rate and median views for each. You probably post at some times more than others, so note how many posts sit in each
block before drawing conclusions. When a block looks strong, post there a few more times to confirm it, and now and then try a block you
rarely use. Over a couple of months, a clear picture emerges.

### 4. Follower changes

Write down your follower count once a week, on the same day. The weekly change tells you more than the total, especially if you also note
what you did differently that week: more replies, a thread, a new topic.

X does not notify you when someone unfollows. If you want to know who left, you can compare follower lists over time or use a tracker that
records new followers and unfollowers each day. Watch for patterns rather than individuals. A cluster of unfollows after a certain kind of
post tells you something; one person leaving usually does not.

You can also look for posts that bring followers. Note your follower count before a post and again a day later. It is rough, since other
things happen at the same time, but over many posts the ones that bring followers stand out.

### Free tools that help

- **A spreadsheet.** Google Sheets or any spreadsheet app handles every calculation here.
- **X itself.** The counts under each post, plus whatever analytics your account type includes.
- **A free engagement rate calculator**, like the one above, for one-off checks.
- **X Lists**, to watch what works for others in your niche without following everyone.
- **A follower tracker**, if you would rather not compare follower lists by hand. Prefer one that never asks for your X password and keeps
  your data in your own browser or account.

### A 20-minute weekly review

1. Add last week's posts to the sheet once they reach your fixed age.
2. Write down your follower count.
3. Find the posts that beat your median engagement rate and note what they have in common.
4. Pick one change for the coming week: a topic, a format or a time block.
5. Next week, check whether it helped.

### Mistakes to avoid

- Judging a post in its first hour.
- Comparing raw likes across posts with very different reach.
- Copying another account's best time or format without testing it.
- Changing five things at once, so you never learn which one mattered.
- Treating one viral post as a strategy.

Measuring this way is not glamorous, but it is honest and free. After a couple of months you will know more
about your own audience than any generic benchmark can tell you.
